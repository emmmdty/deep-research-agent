## Executive Summary

- The 1977 New York City blackout was a power failure triggered by a thunderstorm on the night of June 13, 1977, plunging the city into darkness for over 24 hours and leading to widespread looting and arson.
- Mayor Abe Beame, who was mayor during the blackout, publicly commented on the violence, vandalism, theft, and discomfort experienced by citizens.
- The blackout's racial undertones were reported to have effectively ended Percy E. Sutton's mayoral candidacy, and Sutton himself referred to looters as "marauders and criminals."
- The Latin term "genius loci" has multiple accepted meanings, including "spirit of place," "guardian spirit of a place," and "genius of the place," with origins in classical Roman religion.
- Several books document the 1977 blackout and related events, including James Goodman's "Blackout" (2003) and Jonathan Mahler's "Ladies and Gentlemen, the Bronx Is Burning" (2005).

## Findings

### The 1977 New York City Blackout: Cause and Immediate Effects

The New York City Blackout of 1977 was a power failure that occurred on the night of June 13, 1977, following a thunderstorm. The city was plunged into darkness for over 24 hours. The blackout involved looting and arson across New York City. The event occurred during a period of stress for the city, characterized by a poor economy and reports of a serial killer calling himself the "Son of Sam."

### Mayor Abe Beame's Response

Mayor Abe Beame was the mayor of New York City during the 1977 blackout. He commented on the event, saying, "We've seen our citizens subjected to violence, vandalism, theft and discomfort."

### Political Consequences: Percy E. Sutton

The racial undertones of the 1977 blackout all but killed the mayoral candidacy of Percy E. Sutton. Sutton called the looters "marauders and criminals."

### The Blackout's Aftermath and Recovery

Six weeks after the 1977 blackout, three quarters of the looted stores were back open. However, a year after the blackout, nine out of ten stores on Broadway in Bushwick were still closed. In contrast, there was no looting in Bushwick during the 2003 blackout.

### Presidential Responses and Media Coverage

The Daily News captured President Ford's response to New York City's request for federal help as "FORD TO CITY: DROP DEAD." President Ford famously refused to bail New York City out of its financial crisis, a response memorialized in that headline. After the 1977 blackout, President Carter refused to declare the city a major disaster area.

### Con Edison and the Cause of the Blackout

Con Edison's critics pointed to "a complex managerial failure" as the root cause of the 1977 blackout. Before 1977, Con Edison's first commandment was "Thou shall not shed load." Con Edison shed Washington Heights and Inwood in the summer of 1999.

### The Debate Framing

The 1977 blackout in New York City involved looting, and the debate about it was framed as a dichotomy between right and left.

### Books Documenting the Blackout and Era

James Goodman is the author of "Blackout" (North Point, 2003), a history of the 1977 blackout in New York City. Jonathan Mahler is the author of "Ladies and Gentlemen, the Bronx Is Burning: 1977, Baseball, Politics, and the Battle for the Soul of a City" (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2005). A separate book titled "The New York City Blackout of 1977" is 58 pages and was published July 12, 2016; it contains four small photographs, including one actually taken during the blackout.

### Justin Torres's Works

Justin Torres's book "Blackouts" is largely confined to a room and feels like a metaphor. Torres's debut novel is "We the Animals," a semi-autobiographical tale of three young brothers.

### Genius Loci: Definitions and Origins

The Latin phrase "genius loci" means "spirit of place" and can refer to the guardian spirit of a place. It is a Latin term meaning the genius of the place, referring to the presiding deity or spirit. Genius loci means the guardian spirit of a place. Originating from classical Roman religion, genius loci refers to the protective spirit of a place. The Romans literally believed that a "genius," or a "guardianspirit," resides in everything and everywhere. The concept of "genius loci" (spirit of place) has meanings ranging from the special atmosphere of a place to the guardian spirit of a place. The term "genius loci" refers to the spirit of the land, distinct from the comfort of institutions. A daemon is defined as a guardian spirit of a place or person.
